Grammar Guide: Conditionals

  • Zero Conditional (Fact): If + Present Simple, Present Simple. (e.g., If you heat water, it boils.)
  • First Conditional (Possible Future): If + Present Simple, Will + Verb. (e.g., If it rains, I will stay home.)
  • Second Conditional (Unreal Present/Future): If + Past Simple, Would + Verb. (e.g., If I had money, I would travel.)
  • Third Conditional (Unreal Past): If + Past Perfect, Would have + Past Participle. (e.g., If I had studied, I would have passed.)
  • Mixed Conditional: Mix of 2nd & 3rd. (e.g., If I had slept earlier [past], I would not be tired now [present].)

Activity 6: Listen, Read & Comprehend

Recent technological advances have made an uncomfortable question increasingly urgent: does the ability to innovate justify the speed of deployment? Artificial intelligence, gene editing and quantum computing promise substantial benefits, yet their dangers cannot be assessed through technical performance alone. The central issue is whether institutions can distribute those benefits while remaining accountable for foreseeable harm.

Generative AI can produce fluent explanations, realistic videos and computer code. Researchers also use it to analyse data and design experiments. However, impressive performance conceals uneven reliability: systems still invent information and struggle with extended tasks. Deepfakes create another danger, enabling fraud and impersonation. If persuasive output is mistaken for verified evidence, convenience may erode the judgement that responsible decisions require.

Meanwhile, AI-assisted gene editing is improving researchers’ ability to predict unwanted changes and design more precise interventions. Such advances could support personalised treatments for genetic diseases. Nevertheless, prediction is not a guarantee. Unintended mutations, delivery difficulties and biased data remain obstacles. If promising therapies are deployed before these limitations are understood, vulnerable patients could bear risks that enthusiastic investors underestimate.

Quantum computing raises a different timing problem. Although its most disruptive security consequences remain prospective, organisations already fear that encrypted information collected today could be decrypted later. Waiting for an unmistakable breakthrough would therefore confuse the arrival of a capability with the beginning of exposure. Preparing safer encryption is not necessarily alarmism; it can be prudent anticipation.

These examples suggest that neither unrestricted acceleration nor blanket prohibition offers a convincing response. Instead, independent testing, proportionate safeguards and meaningful public scrutiny should accompany innovation. A technology may be beneficial in principle yet irresponsible in a particular setting. Genuine progress requires asking not merely whether a system works, but who benefits, who absorbs failure, and who can challenge its use.
